## Before

The researchers on my team told me the same things in different words: spread thin, in the weeds, reactive, no time for the research they thought mattered. One of them put it simply. They wanted research to tell them what to research.

The symptoms showed up at three levels. Individuals were burning out and couldn't connect their projects to their own growth. The team couldn't do foundational work and couldn't say no to new requests. The organization planned inconsistently and had no data on what research was producing, which made every resourcing conversation hard.

## What I did

Prioritization takes the whole team, including product, so I built a process the product team could see and take part in:

1. Generate the list of candidate projects for the quarter.
2. Open a quarterly research planning issue, visible to the whole product team.
3. Run every candidate through a simple research priority calculator.
4. Add the agreed projects to the plan.
5. New requests go through the calculator when they arrive, not at the end of the quarter.

The rules that made it work: plan for a fixed period, keep the plan where the product team already works, get product involved in the scoring, point back to the plan every time someone asks to add work, retro on the process on a set cadence, keep the calculator simple, and override it when judgment says to. The calculator supports the decision. It doesn't make it.

## What changed

Research throughput became visible for the first time. Resourcing conversations moved from anecdote to data. Research operations could forecast workload. The surprise was that researchers started collaborating more, because they could see each other's plans.
