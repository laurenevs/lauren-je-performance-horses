## Before

Five product lines were each starting to add AI capability at roughly the same time. Each squad was about to name its own agent, design its own approval step and invent its own way of showing what the agent had done. Customers who use more than one product would have had to learn five versions of the same idea.

This was a direction problem, not a design problem. No single squad was doing anything wrong. Nobody owned the shared answer, and every tradeoff that crossed a product line had to escalate to get settled.

There was groundwork to build on. I had already written the experience strategy and design principles for the company's largest product area, reframing it from a system of record, where people store work, to a system of action, where the product helps them do it. Agentic capability was the obvious next step in that argument.

## What I did

I wrote a framework in three parts, each answering a different question a designer runs into.

**A three-tier model** for how AI capability shows up in the product. It answers: how much is the agent doing here, and how much is the person?

**Six shared primitives**, designed once and reused across all five product lines, so squads don't each invent their own agent vocabulary. It answers: what does this look like, and has someone already designed it?

**Experience principles and a now, next, later prioritization.** It answers: when two good options conflict, which one wins, and does it have to win this quarter? This is the part that lets designers settle tradeoffs in the room instead of escalating them.

Research shaped it directly. I directed the program that set the company's position on AI trust, and its central finding was that people trust an agent based on what they see it do, not on what it claims it can do. So the primitives make the agent's work visible and easy to check, instead of relying on persuasion.

Designing the primitives once, centrally, was a deliberate trade. Squads lose a little freedom and gain consistency, speed and a shared language with engineering. I made that trade explicit up front instead of letting it come out in design reviews.

## What changed

The framework now guides design direction across all five product lines. The team aligned around the principles, and the designs got more concrete and more consistent from one product line to the next.

It's the clearest example of me setting product direction instead of supporting it.
