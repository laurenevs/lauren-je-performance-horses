## Before

Five product lines were each starting to add AI capability at roughly the same time. Each squad was about to name its own agent, design its own approval step and invent its own way of showing what the agent had done. Customers who use more than one product would have had to learn five versions of the same idea.

This was a direction problem, not a design problem. No single squad was doing anything wrong. Nobody owned the shared answer.

There was groundwork to build on. I had already written the experience strategy and design principles for a major product area, reframing it from a system of record, where people store work, to a system of action, where the product helps them do it. Agentic capability was the obvious next step in that argument.

## What I did

I wrote a three-tier model for how AI capability shows up in the product, and defined six shared primitives that every product line uses instead of building its own.

The trust research described in [Changing how a design and research org works with AI](/ux/work/ai-operating-model/) shaped the framework directly. If people trust an agent based on what they see it do, the primitives have to make the agent's work visible and easy to check, and they can't depend on persuasion.

Designing the primitives once, centrally, was a deliberate trade. Squads lose a little freedom and gain consistency, speed and a shared language with engineering. I made that trade explicit up front instead of letting it come out in design reviews.

## What changed

The framework now guides product direction across a major product portfolio. It's the clearest example of me setting product direction instead of supporting it.
