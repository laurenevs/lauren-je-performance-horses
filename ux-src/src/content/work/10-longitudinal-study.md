## Before

Customer satisfaction had been a quarterly objective for a year and hadn't moved, despite steady feature releases. Discovery had focused mostly on one persona. Most research was interviews: attitudinal, with almost no observation.

The data had a clear pointer. Testers, the people doing the hands-on work, were consistently less satisfied than managers. They were also rarely the ones who bought or configured the product.

## What I did

A short exploratory analysis to agree on goals with design and product. Then a longitudinal study: ten testers who met set criteria, meeting every two weeks for six months. Across that time we ran open-ended interviews, attitudinal surveys, recorded observations of real working sessions, and concept validations.

The design let us learn something from one participant and validate it with another within days. That speed was the main benefit of the format.

## What we found

Many small frictions that added up for people who spend hours a day in the product. New features weren't reaching testers in larger organizations. Some testers had started working around the product entirely, mostly in spreadsheets, and the spreadsheet workflow carried its own pain. Observation surfaced bugs and performance problems that interviews never would have.

## What changed

The findings shaped the roadmap and the research that followed for years, and fed design decisions in near real time. Product satisfaction scores improved overall and among testers. The study protocol was reused in other product areas.

## What I would do differently

Six months was too long. I'd expand the sample for new feature work, lean further into observation and build evaluative methods into the study from the start.
